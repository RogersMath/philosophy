Introduction to Philosophy on One Sheet

The big questions, the best-known answers, and the sources behind them
Public domain

A one-semester course on two sides. Each card asks one big question and gives the best-known answers. Key terms are in bold. Small numbers point to the sources on the back.

1 How to Think: Arguments

Logic: the study of good reasoning

Philosophy means asking big questions and giving reasons for your answers. In Plato’s account of his trial, Socrates said that an unexamined life is not worth living.1 An argument is a set of reasons, called premises, that support a conclusion. An argument is valid if the conclusion must be true whenever the premises are true. It is sound if it is valid and the premises really are true. To test an argument, look for a false premise or a gap in the logic.

2 What We Can Know

Epistemology: the study of knowledge

Descartes asked if anything is beyond doubt. He imagined a powerful trickster fooling him about everything, but he could not doubt that he was thinking.2 Hume noted that we expect the future to match the past, yet we cannot prove that it will.3 For a long time, knowledge was defined as justified true belief, which means believing something true for good reasons. In 1963, Gettier described cases where a belief is justified and true only by luck, so it does not seem to be knowledge.4 Philosophers still disagree about what knowledge requires.

3 Mind and Self

Philosophy of mind: minds and selves

Descartes argued that the mind and the body are two different kinds of thing. This view is called dualism.2 Physicalism is the view that everything is physical. Nagel argued that a full physical description of a bat still seems to leave out what it is like to be a bat.5 Chalmers called this puzzle the hard problem of consciousness: why does activity in the brain come with felt experience at all?6 Locke said you stay the same person over time through memory and awareness, not through having the same body.7

4 Free Will

Metaphysics: what reality is like

Determinism is the claim that earlier events and the laws of nature fix everything that happens later. Does that leave room for free will? In this debate, libertarians say we have free will, so determinism must be false. Hard determinists say determinism is true, so we lack free will. Compatibilists say both can be true. Hume argued that acting freely means acting on your own wishes without being forced.3 Frankfurt described cases where a person could not have done otherwise, yet still seems responsible for the choice.8

5 God and Evil

Philosophy of religion: thinking about God

Aquinas gave five arguments for the existence of God. One of them reasons from chains of causes back to a first cause.9 The problem of evil asks how a God who is all-powerful and all-good could allow suffering. Mackie argued that three claims cannot all be true: God is all-powerful, God is all-good, and evil exists.10 Defenders of belief reply in many ways. One reply is that a world with free choices is worth the risk that some people will choose evil.

6 Right and Wrong

Ethics: what is right and wrong

Three main theories explain what makes acts right. Virtue ethics says to build good character, such as courage, which sits between cowardice and recklessness.11 Deontology says some acts are required or forbidden. Kant said to act only on rules you could want everyone to follow.12 Consequentialism says the right act produces the best results. Mill’s version, utilitarianism, counts overall happiness.13 Trolley cases test the theories. Should you switch a runaway trolley to kill one person instead of five? Should you push a large man off a bridge to stop it?14

7 Justice and Government

Political philosophy: rights and power

Why should anyone obey a government? Hobbes said that life with no government would mean constant fear, so people give up some freedom to a ruler in return for safety.15 Locke said people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and a government that violates them can lose its authority.16 Rawls asked which rules we would choose if we did not know our own place in society. He called this test the veil of ignorance. He argued that we would choose equal basic liberties and protect the people who are worst off.17
